# Methods Library

The behavioural-economics toolkit as the DX guide records it, plus the Hook Model.

> **Nudge** — any aspect of the choice architecture that alters behaviour in a predictable way
> without forbidding any options or significantly changing economic incentives. Nudges are easy
> and cheap to avoid, such as placing fruit at eye level in a cafeteria.
>
> **Libertarian paternalism** — a philosophy that aims to influence choices in ways that make
> choosers better off, **as judged by themselves**, while preserving freedom of choice.

That second definition is the test. "As judged by themselves" and "easy and cheap to avoid" are
what separate a nudge from a manipulation, and they are the standard every method below is held to.

> **Read this alongside `ethics.md`.** This library is source material, catalogued from general
> practice. Several techniques in it are **banned at Nusuk** — the "At Nusuk" line on each method
> is the governing rule, and it wins over the generic technique every time.

## 1 · Choice architecture

The design of the different ways choices can be presented, and the impact of that presentation on
decision-making.

**Psychology** — decision fatigue (too many choices overwhelm, causing poor decisions or
paralysis) · simplification (reducing complexity helps users decide quickly with less effort).

**Applications**
- **Default options** — beneficial defaults that guide without restricting. People stick with
  defaults through inertia and a desire to avoid complex decisions.
- **Partitioning choices** — grouping similar options. Categorising reduces cognitive load.
- **Framing effects** — presenting choices to highlight benefits ("80% lean" vs "20% fat").

**Tips** — clear, simple language and visuals · limit the number of options · highlight the most
beneficial option as default or recommended.

**At Nusuk** — fully endorsed, with one condition: **defaults are starting points and must be
editable.** "Today's 3 actions, not 30" is this method. See pattern 1.

---

## 2 · Commitment devices

Tools that help people lock into a course of action aligned with long-term goals.

**Psychology** — present bias (immediate rewards beat long-term benefits) · cognitive dissonance
(commitment devices reduce the discomfort of acting against one's goals).

**Applications** — automatic savings plans ("Save More Tomorrow") · fitness pledges with reminders
and progress tracking · long-term subscriptions that reduce decision points.

**Tips** — let users set goals and track progress · use reminders · offer incentives for long-term
consistency.

**At Nusuk** — **reframed as "covenant", and softened.** Soft commitments — "set a gentle
reminder" — never "pledge". Public commitment and social accountability are *not* imported: the
risk named in pattern 5 is commitments that feel like an obligation to the app. If–then
implementation intentions are the preferred form.

---

## 3 · Feedback

Information about a user's actions, so they understand consequences and adjust.

**Psychology** — operant conditioning (behaviour shaped by reinforcement) · immediate feedback
beats delayed, because it creates a clear action→outcome link.

**Applications** — progress indicators · real-time notifications · periodic performance reviews
(weekly summaries, monthly reports).

**Tips** — real-time feedback on key actions · visual aids for progress · constructive feedback
that shows how to improve.

**At Nusuk** — endorsed with the competitive edge removed. Progress indicators are
**non-competitive**. "You're on track", never "you're winning". The weekly summary is framed as
**reassurance, not a performance review** — a direct inversion of the generic application above.
**Celebrate returning more than streaks.**

---

## 4 · Framing effects

How presentation influences decisions.

**Psychology** — prospect theory (losses and gains are evaluated differently; people avoid risk
under a positive frame and seek it under a negative one) · positive vs negative wording changes
decisions even when the information is identical.

**Applications** — health messaging ("80% survival" vs "20% mortality") · product descriptions
emphasising benefits · framing annual subscriptions as savings.

**Tips** — frame toward positive outcomes · use positive language · test framings.

**At Nusuk** — endorsed, and load-bearing. Positive, humble framing: **guidance, mercy,
continuity.** End every session with calm closure. **Fear framing is out** — it lifts action
short-term and damages trust long-term. The words that execute a framing decision belong to
the writing job (`writing.md`).

---

## 5 · Scarcity and urgency

Limited availability prompting quicker decisions.

**Psychology** — scarcity principle (limited things become more desirable) · urgency effect (time
limits raise perceived value and pressure to act).

**Generic applications** — "Only 2 items left!" · "Sale ends in 2 hours!" · limited-time discounts
· countdown timers and stock indicators.

**At Nusuk — the sharpest divergence in this library.**
> Use scarcity **only for operational truth** — capacity, scheduling constraints.
> **Never use scarcity to emotionally pressure spiritual decisions.**

Countdown timers manufactured to lift conversion are out. A permit window closing on a real date
is fact and may be stated plainly. The test: **does the deadline exist without the campaign?**

---

## 6 · Social proof

People look to others' behaviour to guide their own.

**Psychology** — social validation (conforming to perceived norms, especially under uncertainty) ·
bandwagon effect.

**Generic applications** — reviews, ratings, testimonials · "Join over 10,000 satisfied
customers" · real-time activity feeds of recent purchases or sign-ups.

**At Nusuk** — narrowed sharply. Social proof only **if true and privacy-safe**: "People in your
city often do X after Fajr". Community reassurance — "You're not alone". **No popularity vanity,
no "trending worship", no follower counts, no real-time activity feeds of other people's
worship.** Named risks: comparison, performative religiosity, exclusion.

---

## 7 · Anchoring

Initial information sets a reference point that shapes later judgements.

**Psychology** — anchoring effect · reference dependence.

**Generic applications** — original price beside discounted price · presenting high-value options
first to make others seem affordable.

**At Nusuk** — repurposed. **Anchors for correctness**: trusted sources, clear sequence. The
anchor establishes what *right* looks like in a rite, not what *expensive* looks like in a
catalogue.

---

## 8 · Decoy effect

An inferior option that makes another look more attractive.

**Psychology** — asymmetric dominance · choice enhancement.

**Generic applications** — three pricing tiers where the middle is engineered to win.

**At Nusuk — banned outright.**
> **No decoys**: never manipulate choices by making one option look stupid.

This is the one method in the library that carries an explicit prohibition in its own Nusuk
pattern page. Named risk: manipulation perception.

---

## 9 · Endowment effect

People value things more once they own them.

**Psychology** — ownership raises perceived value · loss aversion.

**Generic applications** — free trials · money-back guarantees.

**At Nusuk** — permitted as **"experience ownership"**: a short trial of a guidance feature so the
value is felt before commitment. Not as a lever for retention pressure.

---

## Further concepts in the guide

**Loss aversion / FOMO** — people prefer avoiding losses to acquiring gains. Generic use is
"don't risk losing your coverage". **At Nusuk this is where the guide is most explicit: avoid
fear-based FOMO — "don't miss reward" is risky.** Loss framing on worship converts guilt into
engagement, and the guide names the consequence as long-term backlash.

**Personalisation** — tailored experiences raise engagement and satisfaction. **At Nusuk** this is
the Intelligent principle: adapt to context to reduce cognitive load, with the user intervening
less. Constrained by privacy and by the autonomy rule.

**Commitment and consistency** — small initial commitments lead to larger ones. **At Nusuk**,
usable for habit formation, but never as an upsell ladder; see method 2.

**Gamification** — game elements (points, levels, challenges, leaderboards) to drive engagement.
**At Nusuk, the most heavily constrained method in the library.** No ranks implying moral
superiority, no leaderboards on worship, no streak shame. What survives is progress that is
private, non-competitive, and framed as reassurance.

**Reciprocity** — people feel obliged to return favours. Usable in service and support contexts;
never to create a sense of debt around worship.

# The Hook Model

A framework for habit-forming products across four phases, cycled repeatedly.

**Trigger** — external (cues in the environment: notifications, icons) and internal (associations
in the mind: emotions, routines). *What brings users to your service?*

**Action** — the simplest behaviour done in anticipation of a reward, driven by ability and
motivation. *What is the simplest action, and how can you make it easier?*

**Variable reward** — rewards that vary to maintain interest. Three types: **rewards of the tribe**
(social), **rewards of the hunt** (material or informational), **rewards of the self** (mastery,
competence, completion). *Are users fulfilled yet left wanting more?*

**Investment** — users put in effort, time, data, or money, which loads the next trigger and
increases the product's value with each use. *What "bit of work" do users invest?*

### The ten implementation practices

1. **Utilising triggers** — external first, internal over time
2. **Simplifying actions** — Fogg Behaviour Model: motivation + ability + trigger must converge
3. **Leveraging variable rewards** — intermittent reinforcement
4. **Encouraging investment** — endowment effect, sunk cost
5. **Cognitive dissonance and consistency** — initial commitments driving further action
6. **Creating feedback loops** — immediate reinforcement
7. **Utilising social proof** — reviews, trending content
8. **Leveraging loss aversion** — limited-time offers, countdowns, FOMO
9. **Personalising experience** — recommendations, customised dashboards
10. **Gamification** — points, levels, challenges, leaderboards

### Using Hook at Nusuk

The Hook Model is in the guide, and it is also the framework most in tension with Nusuk's ethics.
Practices **3, 5, 7, 8 and 10** as written — intermittent variable reward, public commitment,
trending content, FOMO, leaderboards — collide with the pattern-page rules. Apply Hook with these
substitutions:

| Hook as written | At Nusuk |
|---|---|
| Variable reward, intermittent reinforcement | **Predictable, calm closure.** Meaning over dopamine. Uncertainty is a cost here, not a hook |
| Rewards of the tribe (social) | Community *reassurance* — "you're not alone" — never ranking |
| Rewards of the self | **The one Nusuk fully endorses**: mastery, competence, completion |
| Loss aversion, FOMO, countdowns | Banned on spiritual decisions |
| Leaderboards, points, levels | No moral scoring, no ranks |
| Streaks as commitment | Celebrate **returning**; the return-after-lapse rate is the health metric |
| Investment to raise switching cost | Investment to raise *personal* value — your intention, your pace |

**The honest version of Hook at Nusuk is: internal trigger (prayer time, routine) → tiny action
(under 30 seconds) → reward of the self (calm confirmation it counted) → investment that
personalises rather than traps.** That cycle is fully consistent with the pattern library. The
rest of Hook is not, and the guide's own risk lines say why.
